## POST 6: THE REGULATORY & ENFORCEMENT MATH (LinkedIn)

The Financial Technology Association sued the CFPB to block consumer protections for Buy Now, Pay Later. They called the CFPB's authority "unprecedented."

Let's check the math.

The CFPB averages 25 enforcement actions per year. The SEC does 700. The FTC does 50-70. State AGs collectively produce thousands. The CFPB's active examinations dropped from 600 to under 70 for 2026.

Meanwhile, the SSA — one director controlling the lifeline for 70 million Americans — follows two internal manuals (POMS and HELIX) classified as "non-binding guidance." Both SSA staff and Administrative Law Judges treat them as binding law. The SSA updates them constantly without notice-and-comment. Disabled claimants have their benefits reduced based on calculations enacted without any APA process. When they appeal, they learn the right to review was already bargained away by colluding parties.

The FHFA — one director overseeing $5.2 trillion in guaranteed mortgage debt and a $190 billion taxpayer bailout still in conservatorship after 17 years.

This is the company the CFPB keeps. And this is the "unprecedented power" the FTA sued to check.

The FTA's core objection: compliance costs to add a clause or extra sentence to clickwrap agreements already in place. Their members generate virtual card numbers for every transaction, charge APRs up to 36%, report to credit bureaus, and hold state lending licenses — but argue they aren't "card issuers" because there's no physical plastic.

The APA has two interesting sections:
- Section 701(a): Agency action committed to agency discretion is unreviewable.
- Section 706(2)(A): Courts shall strike down action that is arbitrary and capricious.

I presume a coin flip determines which interpretation applies.

Seven state attorneys general noticed. Connecticut, California, Colorado, Illinois, Minnesota, North Carolina, and Wisconsin launched a joint inquiry in December 2025 demanding exactly what the CFPB's rule would have required.

The FTA won the lawsuit. The states are filling the vacuum.

And somewhere in that vacuum is a consumer who reported fraud in sixty-two minutes, received an automated denial in eighty-six, got threatened with a countersuit by managing counsel, and watched the CFPB auto-close a complaint based on material misrepresentations.
